# THE MESSAGE

Read this before you look at any reference site. If the page you build does not carry
this, it does not matter how well it is built.

## The one sentence

**PitchGenius makes a salesperson better at the human part of selling. It does not sell for them.**

Founder's own words, on the call, when asked what the brand is:
> "our branding has really got to be Human plus AI."
> "Enhance humans — not replace them."
> "Something very human, creative — and very, very technology. That's what I like."

## The message is the design, not the copy

Say this out loud: **the message is not a headline. The message is what the page looks
like.** Two pages can carry the same words and say opposite things.

A page that says "human + AI" in a headline, over a dark navy gradient with four cyan
icons and a 3D orb, is saying *machine*. It failed before anyone read it.

The page has to LOOK like: humans + AI + technology + sales people.

Concretely, that means the page contains, visibly:

- **People.** Real faces, real hands, real rooms. A rep listening. A team at a table.
  Someone on a call at 6pm with the office lights off. Not avatars, not silhouettes,
  not 3D characters, not "diverse smiling stock office."
- **Something made by a hand.** Ink, paint, watercolour, pencil, paper grain, a texture
  that could not have been produced by a CSS gradient. Used as a layer, not as
  wallpaper.
- **Real product surface.** The actual live-call card, the actual readiness numbers.
  Technology shown as evidence, not as decoration.
- **Warmth against precision.** The human parts are warm and imperfect. The product
  parts are cold, precise, monospaced, numeric. The tension between those two is the
  brand. A page that is warm everywhere is an art gallery. A page that is cold
  everywhere is every other AI SaaS site.

If you strip every word off your page and show it to a stranger, they should say
"something about people, and something technical." If they say "AI startup," you built
the median.

## The two buyers, and the two things they must feel

The page has to work for two very different readers in one scroll. Do not write two
pages. Sequence them.

### 1. The rep — sells to feeling. Give them an UNFAIR ADVANTAGE.

The word is the founder's own and it is the emotional promise: the rep walks into the
call knowing something the other reps do not, and knowing it *while there is still time
to use it*.

What the rep feels reading a good version of this page:
- "I would stop losing deals I could have saved."
- "I would not have to guess whether they are actually interested."
- "I would stop replaying the call afterwards wondering what I missed."

What the rep is afraid of, and you must answer it on the page:
- "Is this thing going to score me and hand the score to my manager?"
  Answer: rep calibration is **private**. The product ships with that promise. Say it.
- "Is this replacing me?"
  Answer: the entire brand. Say it where they can see it, not in the footer.

Never make the rep the problem. The rep is the hero. PitchGenius is the guide.

### 2. The manager / VP Sales — buys with money. Give them the ARITHMETIC.

The rep feels. The manager signs. The manager does not care that it "reads the room."
He cares about four numbers and you must put them on the page in his language:

- **Win rate.** More deals closed out of the same pipeline. Nothing new to buy, no more
  headcount — the same reps, more closes.
- **Ramp time.** A new rep performs like a good rep in weeks instead of quarters,
  because the coaching happens during the call instead of in a Monday review.
- **Forecast accuracy.** Buyer readiness is scored on every call, so the pipeline stops
  being what reps *hope* and starts being what buyers *showed*.
- **Coaching capacity.** One manager can not sit on 40 calls a week. This sits on all
  of them.

And the competitive line, which is the manager's real fear:
**Your competitor's rep is going to have this. The question is whether yours does
first.**

Rule: every claim of this kind on the page must be phrased as a mechanism, not a
percentage, unless we have the measurement. We do not have customer numbers yet. Write
"the same reps close more of the same pipeline," not "37% higher win rate." A fake
number destroys the page for exactly the buyer you were trying to convince.

## The three moments of the product — FACTS, not a section list

**CORRECTED 2026-08-14 — this was the exact line that broke the whole project.** It used
to say "this is the page's spine," and every one of 16 pages built on 16 structurally
different reference sites (a typography-only manifesto, an animated IDE demo, a
photography-led banking site, a whimsical 3D-object hero) came back with the identical
section sequence: hook → before/during/after → "doesn't start from memory" → arithmetic
→ "calibration is private" → FAQ → dark CTA. Sixteen different references, one template.
That happened because this content list read as more concrete and easier to follow than
RULES.md's RECLOTHE instruction to copy the reference's actual structure, so it won.

Before/during/after, the manager arithmetic, and the privacy promise below are FACTS you
must get onto the page somewhere, in some form. They are **not** a required section, not
a required heading, not a required order, and not a required count of sections. Your
reference site has its own real section count and order — read from `./ref/EXTRACT.md`.
Fold these facts into whatever sections it actually has. If the reference has 6 sections,
your page should end up with roughly 6 sections, not 10 because you added one for each
item below. If the reference has no separate "for the manager" section at all, put the
arithmetic in a sentence inside an existing section, or cut it — do not invent a new
section shape to house content the reference wouldn't structurally have room for.

Every version of this page tells one story in three beats. This is the product and it
is also the narrative — but "story in three beats" describes what happens on a call, not
how many sections your page needs.

1. **Before the call** — you know who you are about to talk to. How they decide, what
   builds their trust, what makes them resist. (Buyer Blueprint)
2. **During the call** — you know what they are feeling as they feel it, and you get
   the exact sentence to say next. (HumanCue™ / Live co-pilot / Buyer Readiness Index)
3. **After the call** — the next call does not start from memory. Evidence, MEDDPICC
   gaps, next moves. (Post-call analysis)

The competitive wedge lives in beat 2 and nowhere else. Everyone records calls.
Everyone summarises calls. **Nobody tells the rep what to say while the buyer is still
in the room.** Verified against Gong, Clari, Clari Copilot and Chorus: none of them
claim live in-conversation psychological reading.

The category sentence, stated flat, no adjectives:
> Conversation intelligence tells you what the buyer said. PitchGenius tells you what
> to say next, while there is still time to change the outcome.

## Story structure (from the branding source we were given)

Source: Martin Zarian, "Neurobranding — what happens in the brain when you tell a
story", `assets/story-branding.md`. Russell brought this to the call as the reference
for *why* the page must be a story. Use its five moves:

1. Don't tell them what you do — show them who you are.
2. Create tension. No problem, no story, no reason to care.
3. The customer is the hero. You are the guide.
4. Repeat the message until it is internalised.
5. People don't remember features. They remember feelings.

Applied here: the tension is the moment in a call where you feel it slipping and you
don't know why. The hero is the rep. The guide is PitchGenius. The repeated message is
*human + AI*. The feeling is *I was in control of that conversation*.

## Tone

- Lowercase-calm, person to person. No exclamation marks. No "revolutionise",
  "seamless", "leverage", "supercharge", "game-changing", "10x".
- No unprovable superlatives. Delete "the only AI that…". Delete "the world's first".
- Short sentences. One idea each.
- Specific beats clever. "Trust is dropping and he hasn't said so" beats "AI-powered
  sentiment intelligence."

## The emotional ceiling (use it once, at the end, or not at all)

Russell, on the call, on what a closed deal actually is:
> "Every closed contract you make as a seller is another piece of life you're building
> for your family."

That is the highest note on the page. It belongs in the final CTA or the closing
statement, said once, quietly, with no icon next to it. If you use it twice it becomes
a slogan and dies.
